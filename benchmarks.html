Benchmark ranking

Which Earth observation benchmarks reliably tell geospatial foundation models apart? For every benchmark with published results, the score gives the share of the differences between models that is real rather than run-to-run noise. Built from 14,600 scores in 52 papers. It ranks benchmarks, not models.

    Questions

    Which benchmarks should I use to evaluate a model?

    Prefer Reliable benchmarks reported by several papers (the Papers column) and included in published suites (Suites). If you need to separate strong models from each other, check the Top-half score: a benchmark can score 0.99 overall yet 0.2 among the better half of models, meaning it mainly separates weak baselines from good models (Kuro Siwo, for example). Benchmarks in the same domain and sensor often rank models similarly; the Evidence map helps choose a varied set.

    What exactly does the score measure?

    On any benchmark, models differ by some amount (the Spread). Part of that difference would change if each evaluation were simply run again with another random seed (the Seed noise). The score is the share of the spread that remains after removing the noise: 1 means all of it is real, 0.5 means half. It follows the definition in S. Wang, GeoFM Benchmark Scoring (2026): score = τ² / (τ² + σ²), where σ² is the seed noise and τ² the stable difference between models. The i next to each column gives the exact computation.

    What is the difference between measured and estimated noise?

    Seed noise can only be measured when a paper reports how much a model's score varies across repeated runs (a "mean ± standard deviation"). This is the case for about a quarter of published comparisons. For the rest, the noise is estimated from the same dataset under the same protocol, or from the typical value for that protocol (about 0.6–0.7 points). Scores based on estimated noise carry the verdicts Likely reliable or Uncertain and are listed after those with measured noise.

    How many runs (seeds) should I use?

    To tell apart two models whose scores differ by Δ points with about 95% confidence, each needs roughly 2 × (2σ / Δ)² runs, where σ is the Seed noise column. Typical seed noise is 0.6–0.9 points, so 3–7 runs resolve a 1-point gap. Noisy benchmarks need many more (DynamicEarthNet, σ ≈ 3 points, about 30–55 runs). Papers that report this information use 5 or 10 runs. Analysis of seed noise by domain.

    Why are there no model rankings here?

    Published results are not comparable enough across papers to rank models reliably: the same model on the same benchmark can differ by several points between papers because of implementation choices. Benchmarks can still be assessed, because each paper compares its models under one shared setup.

    Where do the data come from, and what is left out?

    A meta-analysis table of 14,600 scores from 52 papers on geospatial foundation models (V. Mancoridis, 2026). Only original results are used: scores copied from other papers, ablation variants, non-foundation baselines and models trained from scratch are removed. A benchmark needs at least one paper comparing four or more models to be scored. Benchmarks marked not in Explorer have published results but no entry yet in the dataset inventory.

    Why are the columns in this order?

    They follow the scoring method: the verdict and score with its interval first, as the method's single summary per benchmark; then the number of models and papers, which shows how much evidence the score rests on; then spread and seed noise, the two quantities the score is computed from; then the top-half score and suite membership. Further diagnostics from the method (signal-to-noise, dataset-specific signal, information share) are under Additional columns.
